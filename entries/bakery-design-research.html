Design Research Methods Lab: The Bakery

October 7, 2026 · UT 330 Interaction Design

Research Subject

What we studied: What determines a neighborhood bakery's opening hours, and what would make a later production or retail window viable?

Scenario: A bakery opens early and closes by mid-afternoon. Some customers ask for later hours, but the owner does not want to extend the current staff's shifts.

Method: Stakeholder and user interviews with AI-simulated participants, followed by a personal review of the interview notes.

In this class, I worked with two other students to understand the bakery's situation from different perspectives before proposing any design solution. We focused on staff schedules, production, customer habits, and the costs of staying open later.

Interview Preparation

We wrote questions for different roles and used a separate ChatGPT (and Claude for Kaisha's interview) simulation for each participant. Kaisha interviewed the owner, Mizuki interviewed the cashier, and I interviewed the baker. We also prepared follow-up customer interviews. I interviewed a college sophomore engineering student.

For the baker, I asked about his schedule, workload, hourly pay, and production planning. For the student, I asked when he visited, what he bought, and whether later hours would change his habits. My questions focused on how the same change might affect employees and customers differently.

  • Baker: What does your typical schedule look like?
  • Baker: How would extending the hours affect your workload and baking plan?
  • Student: When do you visit the bakery and why?
  • Student: Would later opening hours change what you buy?

Interview Records

Below are selected records from the AI-simulated interviews. I kept the wording from our records and chose passages that we highlighted as important.

Bakery Owner - interviewed by Kaisha

"Counter-wise, it's the morning. We open at 7, and from then until about 9:30 is the rush: coffee, croissants, morning buns, people on their way to work."

"But honestly, if you're asking where the money comes from, the wholesale side is a big chunk too, and that isn't tied to the counter at all."

"The real risk to me isn't the materials themselves; it's making things nobody buys."

The owner's answers suggested that the business is built around morning retail demand and early wholesale deliveries. She was unsure whether requests for later hours would turn into enough sales.

Cashier - interviewed by Mizuki

"I’m used to being done around 2, so staying until 5 or 6 would mean arranging different things around work."

"Sometimes, yes. I can stay an extra hour or two with some notice, especially if someone calls out. But I can’t regularly work longer shifts because I have other commitments after work."

The cashier was open to occasional changes but needed a predictable schedule. Keeping the store open longer would not necessarily mean that the same employees could cover the extra hours.

Baker (Marcus) - interviewed by me

"I usually start around 4:30 or 5 in the morning, a couple hours before we open."

"I’d also worry about whether we’d be expected to keep producing fresh stuff throughout the afternoon."

"If there were a separate later shift, though, that would feel different. I wouldn’t necessarily mind handing things off to someone else."

"But more money wouldn’t really make me want to turn my current shift into a much longer one."

This interview showed me that retail hours and production hours are connected, but they are not the same. The baker also needs time for cleaning and preparing the next day's products. A later shift could be possible, but it would require planning the handoff and production schedule.

College Student (Ethan) - interviewed by me

"There have definitely been times when I wanted to stop by after my last class, like around 4:30 or 5, but by then they’re usually already closed."

"I probably wouldn’t spend $10 there every day, but once or twice a week feels reasonable to me."

"For me, it would make some difference, but probably not a huge difference."

"But I probably wouldn’t think of the bakery as my regular dinner place."

The student liked the idea of stopping by after class, but he did not expect to visit every day or use the bakery as a regular dinner place. This helped me see the difference between a convenient service and a service people would use frequently.

Personal Synthesis

After reviewing our highlighted interview notes, I grouped the main observations into related themes. This is my own synthesis of the simulation records done individually.

Morning-Centered Operations

The owner described a morning sales rush and wholesale deliveries before opening. The baker also begins work hours before customers arrive. The current schedule is shaped by both retail and production needs.

Staffing & Workload

The baker did not want a much longer shift, even with more pay. The cashier could sometimes stay later but could not do so regularly. A separate shift would need a clear handoff and predictable staffing.

Customer Demand & Timing

The owner noticed possible demand from younger residents, and the student sometimes wanted to visit after class. However, the student expected only occasional visits. Interest in later hours does not show how many purchases would actually happen.

Production, Freshness & Waste

The baker would need to decide how much to prepare for an uncertain evening period. The owner was concerned about producing items that might not sell. Later hours could affect freshness, batch planning, and food waste.

Costs & Space

The owner raised concerns about labor, ingredient costs, and the limited kitchen space. Afternoon production could overlap with preparation for the next morning, so longer hours would involve more than keeping the front counter open.

What I learned

I noticed that some of my questions asked participants to predict how they would feel or behave. If I repeated this exercise with real participants, I would ask more about specific past experiences, such as the last time a baker changed a production plan or a customer arrived after closing. That would help me understand what people actually did, rather than only what they expected to do.

Another thing I noticed was the difference between interviewing AI-simulated participants and real people. If we were conducting this research with real participants, I think our approach would be quite different. We would probably visit the bakery first, observe its daily operations, and learn more about the physical space, staff routines, and customer behavior before preparing our interview questions. This would give us a more complete understanding of the context. I also felt that the AI's responses were sometimes influenced by how I phrased my questions, especially when I suggested a possible answer. While real participants can also be influenced by leading questions, I think they would be more likely to bring up unexpected experiences or perspectives that we had not considered. This made me realize the importance of observation and open-ended questions in real-world design research.
